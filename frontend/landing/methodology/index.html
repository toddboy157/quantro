Methodology

How the numbers on the dashboard are actually computed

No black box. Every figure traces back to a real options chain and a documented calculation — here's the whole pipeline, formulas included.

1. The data

Every refresh cycle, Quantro pulls the full live options chain for each underlying it tracks: every strike, every expiry, open interest, implied volatility, and (where the vendor provides them) greeks. Index and equity/ETF options are treated the same way once the chain is in hand.

2. Recomputing the greeks

Rather than trusting a vendor's greeks directly, Quantro recomputes delta and gamma itself with a standard Black-Scholes model, so every downstream number is consistent — including at hypothetical spot levels the vendor never actually quoted (needed for the zero-gamma calculation below).

d1 = [ln(S/K) + (r − q + σ²/2)·T] / (σ·√T) Γ = φ(d1) / (S·σ·√T) Δ_call = N(d1) Δ_put = N(d1) − 1

where S is spot, K is strike, T is time to expiry in years, σ is implied volatility, r is the risk-free rate, q is the dividend yield (assumed 0), φ is the standard normal density, and N is the standard normal CDF.

3. Gamma exposure (GEX) and delta exposure (DEX)

Each contract's gamma is weighted by its open interest and contract size, then signed according to the industry-standard dealer-positioning convention: customers are assumed to be net buyers of calls and puts, which means dealers who take the other side of that flow are net short calls and short puts. A dealer who is short a call hedges by buying the underlying as it rises — a stabilizing, "long gamma" flow. That convention is what turns call open interest into positive GEX and put open interest into negative GEX below.

Contract GEX = Γ × OpenInterest × 100 × Spot² × 0.01 (sign: + for calls, − for puts) Net GEX (strike) = Σ call GEX + Σ put GEX, at that strike Net GEX (total) = Σ over every strike and expiry

DEX follows the same shape, substituting delta for gamma and dropping the extra factor of spot (delta exposure scales linearly with spot movement, not quadratically like gamma):

Contract DEX = Δ × OpenInterest × 100 × Spot (sign: + for calls, − for puts)

4. Dealer walls

The call wall is the strike with the highest positive net GEX; the put wall is the strike with the lowest (most negative) net GEX. These are the strikes where dealer hedging flow is theoretically largest in magnitude — in practice, levels price tends to gravitate toward, pin against, or bounce from as expiry approaches and dealers rebalance.

5. The zero-gamma flip level

Net GEX is a function of spot, not just a single number — as spot moves, every contract's gamma (and therefore every contract's GEX) changes too. The zero-gamma level is the spot price at which total net GEX would cross from positive to negative. Quantro finds it by actually re-pricing the whole chain's gamma at a grid of hypothetical spot levels spanning ±12% around the current spot (61 points), then linearly interpolating to the exact level where the sign flips.

Why it matters: when spot is above zero-gamma, dealers are net long gamma and tend to hedge in a way that dampens volatility (buy dips, sell rips). When spot is below zero-gamma, dealers are net short gamma, and hedging flow tends to amplify moves in whichever direction price is already going — the "negative gamma pocket" you'll sometimes see called out on the dashboard.

6. Refresh cadence

The dashboard polls the backend roughly every 2 seconds. Under the hood, that doesn't mean every input changes every tick — real markets don't work that way either: exchanges publish official open interest once per session, and even paid vendors don't refresh it tick-by-tick. Quantro's simulated provider mirrors this deliberately (OI regenerates every ~2 minutes, IV every ~30s, spot moves every tick); the live Massive/Polygon connector simply reflects whatever cadence the vendor itself updates on.

Important

This is positioning data, not a trading signal. Walls and the zero-gamma level describe options market structure as it exists right now, computed from a documented, inspectable formula — they are not predictions, price targets, or investment advice.

The dealer-short hedging convention above is a standard industry assumption used across the positioning-data industry, not a verified fact about any specific market maker's actual book. See the full disclaimer for more.
